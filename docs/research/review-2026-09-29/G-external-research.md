# External Research: State of the Art for Human-Like, Low-Latency, Proactive Windows Voice Assistants (2025-2026)

Compiled for Yuki (Pipecat 1.11, Deepgram Flux STT, Claude Haiku 4.5 talker, Claude Sonnet 5 on Bedrock thinker, Gemini 3.8 Flash-Lite TTS / Deepgram Aura-2 fallback, Windows UI Automation). Research date: 2026-09-29. Excludes speech-to-speech live APIs, keystroke/mouse logging, other-party call recording, and heavy always-on GPU load.

## 1. Turn-Taking & Responsiveness

### 1.1 Deepgram Flux EagerEndOfTurn / TurnResumed (speculative STT-triggered generation)
**What it is:** Flux's turn detection is built into the STT model itself and emits a state-machine event stream. `EagerEndOfTurn` fires speculatively — the model believes the speaker is done — so a consumer can start LLM inference immediately; `TurnResumed` retracts it if the speaker keeps talking, and the in-flight LLM call is cancelled.
**Evidence:** With `eager_eot_threshold` set to 0.3–0.5, Deepgram reports `EagerEndOfTurn` fires 150–250ms earlier than the final `EndOfTurn`, at a cost of 50–70% more LLM calls (most cancelled). Threshold range is 0.3–0.9; Deepgram recommends 0.4–0.5 for lowest perceived latency.
**Application to Yuki:** Directly wire Flux's `EagerEndOfTurn` to speculatively start the Haiku 4.5 talker call before the user has technically finished; cancel-on-`TurnResumed`. Since Haiku is already fast and cheap, the 50-70% extra call volume is a good trade for shaving ~200ms off perceived response time — this is the single highest-leverage, lowest-effort fix for Yuki's "say→plan→execute" feel.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Pure STT-side config change, no new model or GPU. Added cost is proportional to wasted Haiku calls (cheap model, so low $ impact); no footprint increase.
**Sources:** [Optimize Voice Agent Latency with Eager End of Turn](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/voice-agent-eager-eot), [Flux state machine](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/state), [Flux configuration](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/flux/configuration), [Introducing Flux STT](https://deepgram.com/learn/introducing-flux-conversational-speech-recognition)

### 1.2 Pipecat Smart Turn v3 (semantic/acoustic turn model) as a second opinion
**What it is:** An open-source (BSD-2) native-audio semantic VAD that classifies "has the speaker finished" from raw waveform (prosody, pace) rather than just silence, using a Whisper-Tiny-based ~8M-param transformer.
**Evidence:** v3 runs in 12ms on modern CPU, ~60ms on a low-cost AWS instance — small enough to co-run alongside Flux with negligible overhead.
**Application to Yuki:** Since Yuki already pays for Flux (which has its own turn detection), Smart Turn v3 is not strictly needed as the primary detector, but is worth evaluating as a cheap cross-check on ambiguous/eager turns (e.g., to gate whether to actually speak on an `EagerEndOfTurn` vs. wait) — it's CPU-only so it doesn't compete with GPU used elsewhere.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** 12-60ms CPU inference, no GPU; open weights, self-hostable.
**Sources:** [Announcing Smart Turn v3, CPU inference in 12ms](https://www.daily.co/blog/announcing-smart-turn-v3-with-cpu-inference-in-just-12ms/), [pipecat-ai/smart-turn-v3 on Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/pipecat-ai/smart-turn-v3)

### 1.3 LiveKit turn-detector model (comparison architecture)
**What it is:** A 135M-parameter SmolLM2-based transformer fine-tuned to predict the probability of an `<|im_end|>` token given the last four conversation turns — i.e., a semantic continuation classifier, same category as Smart Turn but text/context-based rather than acoustic.
**Evidence:** 98.8% accuracy on true "turn complete" and 87.5% on true "turn incomplete"; LiveKit separately reports this class of model cuts voice-AI interruptions by 39% vs VAD-only baselines.
**Application to Yuki:** Not something to adopt wholesale (LiveKit-specific), but validates the general pattern — Yuki should treat Flux's turn signal as one input and optionally combine with a lightweight text-based completion classifier over the last few turns for cases where prosody alone is ambiguous (e.g., user trailing off mid list).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** 135M params — heavier than Smart Turn v3; would need to run on CPU/small GPU slice, evaluate before adopting given Yuki's "must not compete with games" constraint.
**Sources:** [LiveKit turn detector docs](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/build/turns/turn-detector/), [Using a transformer to improve end-of-turn detection](https://livekit.com/blog/using-a-transformer-to-improve-end-of-turn-detection), [Improved end-of-turn model cuts interruptions 39%](https://livekit.com/blog/improved-end-of-turn-model-cuts-voice-ai-interruptions-39)

### 1.4 The ~200-300ms human turn-taking gap and perception thresholds
**What it is:** Cross-linguistic conversation-analysis research (Stivers et al., PNAS 2009, 10 languages/5 continents) found the modal gap between one speaker stopping and the next starting is ~200ms, remarkably constant across cultures despite language-production latencies of 600-1500ms — implying humans predict turn-ends before they happen, not react after.
**Evidence:** Beyond ~300ms delay is perceived unconsciously as "off"; beyond ~500ms consciously noticed; beyond ~1s, satisfaction/abandonment degrades sharply (cited figure: 40%+ abandonment spike). Industry target for voice agents: P50 time-to-first-audio <800ms, P95 <1.5s.
**Application to Yuki:** This is the hard latency budget Yuki's whole pipeline (Flux EOT → Haiku → Gemini TTS first chunk) must hit for "talks like a human," not just "responds correctly." Use it as the north-star metric, and instrument end-to-end p50/p95 explicitly rather than optimizing individual components in isolation.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** N/A — a target, not a technique.
**Sources:** [You Have 200 Milliseconds Before AI Feels Wrong](https://liveinthefuture.org/stories/ai-latency-200ms-conversational-threshold), [Timing in Conversation, Journal of Cognition](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.268), [Voice AI Latency: What's Fast, What's Slow](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-ai-latency-whats-fast-whats-slow-how-to-fix-it)

### 1.5 Prompt caching (Anthropic/Bedrock) to cut talker TTFT
**What it is:** Cache-control checkpoints on static prompt prefixes (system prompt, tool schemas, persona) so repeated calls skip re-processing those tokens.
**Evidence:** Anthropic/AWS report up to 85% latency reduction and 90% cost reduction on cached prefixes; default cache TTL 5 minutes, extendable to 1 hour. Both Claude Sonnet 5 and (per AWS) Claude Haiku-class models support this on Bedrock as of 2026, via the `converse()` API with `cache_control` (not the older `invoke_model()` path).
**Application to Yuki:** Yuki's talker (Haiku 4.5) and thinker (Sonnet 5 on Bedrock) both carry large, mostly-static system prompts (persona, tool defs, memory-retrieval scaffolding). Cache these; this is close to "free" latency/cost reduction and should be table stakes already, but flag it explicitly since it's easy to regress by putting per-turn dynamic content (e.g. live screen text) before the cache boundary instead of after it.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Pure API-call configuration; no infra changes. Must ensure Bedrock `converse()` is used, not `invoke_model()`.
**Sources:** [Effectively use prompt caching on Amazon Bedrock (AWS blog)](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/effectively-use-prompt-caching-on-amazon-bedrock), [Anthropic: prompt caching](https://www.anthropic.com/news/prompt-caching), [Prompt caching with Bedrock and Anthropic, demystified](https://medium.com/@niklas-palm/prompt-caching-with-bedrock-and-anthropic-models-demystified-72261d269845)

### 1.6 Streaming TTS from first clause + pipeline-stage overlap
**What it is:** Rather than waiting for the full LLM turn to generate, split at sentence/clause boundaries and start TTS synthesis on segment 1 while segment 2 is still being generated by the LLM; stream the resulting audio in chunks so playback starts before synthesis of the whole utterance finishes.
**Evidence:** Industry consensus figure: P50 time-to-first-audio target <800ms, P95 <1.5s, achieved specifically by overlapping STT-end/LLM-start/TTS-start rather than running them serially.
**Application to Yuki:** Confirm Pipecat's aggregator is chunking Haiku's streamed output at sentence boundaries before handing to Gemini TTS (Pipecat 1.11 supports this pattern natively via its LLM-to-TTS text aggregators) — if Yuki currently waits for a full Haiku response before calling TTS, this is a major, concrete fix.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** No new model; pipeline wiring only. Slight risk of TTS mispronouncing sentence fragments if boundaries are cut too aggressively — tune chunk size.
**Sources:** [Time to First Audio: Measuring and Reducing TTS Latency (Gradium)](https://gradium.ai/blog/time-to-first-audio), [Voice agent latency optimization (ElevenLabs)](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/voice-agent-latency-optimization), [Streaming TTS: why time-to-first-audio decides UX](https://soniox.com/wiki/streaming-tts)

### 1.7 Backchanneling in both directions
**What it is:** Short listener-signal utterances — "mm-hm", "yeah", "okay" — that (a) the assistant emits while the user is still talking to show it's listening, and (b) the assistant should tolerate from the user without treating them as a full interruption (i.e., classify "mhm" as a continuer, not a barge-in, and keep speaking through it).
**Evidence:** Backchannel classification is now a documented test category for voice-agent QA (e.g. Retell/Hamming test suites: playback pauses, the short utterance is transcribed and classified, and if it's only an acknowledgement, playback resumes from exactly where it stopped rather than restarting).
**Application to Yuki:** Two concrete builds: (1) When the *thinker* is executing a long UIA task, have the *talker* emit occasional short backchannel-style progress tokens rather than full sentences (see Section 2). (2) Add a classifier step (can be a cheap Haiku call or regex+VAD heuristic) so a user saying "yeah/okay/mm" while Yuki is mid-sentence doesn't trigger a full barge-in/cancel.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Minimal — a short-utterance classifier is cheap; must be fast enough (<100ms) to not itself introduce lag in the interruption path.
**Sources:** [What is Backchanneling? (Retell AI)](https://www.retellai.com/blog/how-backchanneling-improves-user-experience-in-ai-powered-voice-agents), [Voice Agent Interruption Handling: Barge-In, Backchannels, Turn Detection (Hamming)](https://hamming.ai/resources/voice-agent-interruption-handling-runbook)

### 1.8 Full-duplex "inner monologue" idea from Moshi (design inspiration only — not adoptable directly)
**What it is:** Kyutai's Moshi is a genuine full-duplex speech-to-speech model (not applicable to Yuki directly since it's a different model family, not a live API — but conceptually adjacent to the excluded category, so treat as *inspiration only*, per the task's framing). Its key trick: it generates a synchronized **text token stream ("inner monologue")** alongside its audio output tokens, word-aligned with the audio, which measurably improves linguistic quality of the generated speech and gives you a free, real-time transcript of what the model is about to say.
**Evidence:** Reported theoretical latency 160ms / ~200ms practical, full-duplex (listens and speaks simultaneously, no turn-taking at the model level).
**Application to Yuki:** The transferable idea for a *cascaded* pipeline is: have Haiku always produce its spoken text as a fast, parallel text stream that both drives TTS *and* is simultaneously available to a lightweight "am I saying something dumb / contradicting the thinker" self-check, rather than treating TTS as a black box consuming final text. Do not attempt to adopt Moshi's actual full-duplex audio architecture — that would reintroduce the excluded "speech-to-speech live model" category and also requires always-on GPU incompatible with Yuki's lightweight constraint.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** N/A as direct adoption; the "parallel text stream" idea costs nothing extra since Haiku already emits text before TTS.
**Sources:** [Moshi: a speech-text foundation model for real-time dialogue (arXiv 2410.00037)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00037), [kyutai-labs/moshi GitHub](https://github.com/kyutai-labs/moshi)

**Flag:** The "filler-word strategy that doesn't sound robotic" ask in the brief is addressed concretely in Section 2 (Vapi's `request-start`/`request-response-delayed` pattern) rather than here, since production evidence for filler-word *design* lives in the tool-call-narration literature, not pure turn-taking literature.

## 2. "Talk While Working" / Dual-Process Architecture

### 2.1 The Talker-Reasoner architecture (DeepMind, arXiv 2410.08328) — direct validation of Yuki's own design
**What it is:** A published dual-process agent architecture that splits into a fast, conversational **Talker** (handles dialogue and quick environment interaction) and a slow, deliberative **Reasoner** (multi-step planning, belief formation), explicitly modeled on System-1/System-2 cognition. This is architecturally almost identical to Yuki's Haiku-talker / Sonnet-thinker split.
**Evidence:** The paper frames and motivates the split with worked examples showing the Talker keeps the conversation flowing in real time while the Reasoner works in the background and updates the Talker's beliefs/context asynchronously.
**Application to Yuki:** Use this paper as the architectural reference to justify (and refine) the existing split: the Reasoner/thinker should not talk directly to the user at all — it should only ever update a shared belief/state object that the Talker reads from and narrates. This reframes "doesn't talk while it works" as a state-sharing problem, not a prompting problem: the talker needs a live feed of the thinker's *intermediate* state (current step, last tool result, confidence), updated continuously, not just a final answer.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Architecture-level guidance, no direct cost; implies adding a shared, frequently-updated state object between the two agent loops (e.g., in-memory pub/sub) which is cheap.
**Sources:** [Agents Thinking Fast and Slow: A Talker-Reasoner Architecture (arXiv 2410.08328)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.08328)

### 2.2 Vapi's tool-message lifecycle (`request-start` / `request-response-delayed` / `request-complete` / `request-failed`) — most concrete production pattern found
**What it is:** Vapi lets you attach canned, pre-authored spoken messages to specific lifecycle events of a tool call: fired the instant the tool starts ("Checking the weather forecast, please wait…"), fired again after ~1s if still running ("Still pulling that, one moment."), and on completion/failure. Crucially, these are played by the platform directly — they do **not** depend on the LLM generating an acknowledgment first, so they're guaranteed to fire and add zero LLM latency.
**Evidence:** Documented as the standard fix for the specific failure mode "without an acknowledgment, the caller hears silence and assumes the agent froze"; recommended timeout config is to fire the delayed-message at ~1s and hard-fail around 6-10s.
**Application to Yuki:** This maps directly onto Yuki's problem. When the thinker kicks off a multi-step UIA task (which can run 10-60+ seconds), the talker should immediately play a pre-authored/cached start line, then at ~3-5s intervals play short, *state-derived* progress lines pulled from the thinker's real intermediate state (see 2.1) rather than generic filler — e.g., "opening Outlook now" → "found the invoice" → "filling in the amount" — each grounded in an actual tool-call event, not a canned loop. This is the concrete implementation of "thinking out loud driven by real tool events."
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Near-zero — these are either pre-synthesized/cached audio clips (best for the very first "starting…" line, since it needs zero latency) or short Haiku-generated lines conditioned on the thinker's last tool-result (cheap, ~100-300ms generation, can be pipelined with TTS streaming from 1.6).
**Sources:** [Function tools (Vapi docs)](https://docs.vapi.ai/tools/custom-tools), [How to Optimize Vapi Voice Agent Latency in 2026: 12 Techniques](https://futureagi.com/blog/how-to-optimize-vapi-latency-2026/)

### 2.3 Pipecat's async function-call machinery (FunctionCallInProgressFrame, deferred results, `TTSSpeakFrame` filler)
**What it is:** Pipecat's actual (not hypothetical) primitives for this problem: a `FunctionCallInProgressFrame` marks a tool call as running; an async tool handler can speak filler via `TTSSpeakFrame(append_to_context=True)` while the call is in flight; when the tool resolves, its result either overwrites a "started" placeholder in the LLM context or — if the conversation has since "moved on" (new user/developer messages arrived) — is appended as a deferred developer message instead of silently dropped.
**Evidence:** This is drawn directly from Pipecat's own GitHub issues/PRs (e.g. PR #5877 "Relay a backend's progress to a speech-to-speech frontend, as it comes", PR #5820, PR #5705, issue #3661) — i.e., primary source, actively-developed code, not marketing copy. Notably, **issue #4492 ("Add support for Tool call Fillers/Acknowledgement messages") is open as of this research**, meaning first-class filler support (equivalent to Vapi's `request-start`) is not yet a built-in Pipecat feature — Yuki would currently have to hand-roll it using `TTSSpeakFrame` inside the async tool handler.
**Application to Yuki:** Since Yuki is already Pipecat-based, this is the mechanism to actually build the progress-narration feature: wrap the thinker's UIA tool calls as async Pipecat function calls, emit `TTSSpeakFrame` lines from inside the handler at meaningful checkpoints, and rely on Pipecat's context-reconciliation logic to keep the talker's conversation state consistent when the user talks over/past the tool call.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Implementation effort (custom wiring), not infra cost; this is currently the highest-value engineering task identified in this section since it directly targets the reported "doesn't talk while it works" complaint.
**Sources:** [Pipecat Function Calling docs](https://docs.pipecat.ai/pipecat/learn/function-calling), [PR #5877](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/pull/5877), [PR #5820](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/pull/5820), [Issue #4492 — tool call fillers (open/unresolved)](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/issues/4492), [Issue #5779 — interruption during tool call can leave it stuck](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/pipecat/issues/5779)

### 2.4 OpenAI Agents SDK guidance: latency budgets force a spoken preamble
**What it is:** OpenAI's own voice-agent prompting guidance states a documented rule of thumb: tool calls under ~2s need no acknowledgment, 2-5s need a spoken preamble, over 5s "breaks the conversation" without one — and explicitly instructs the model *not* to narrate tool mechanics ("don't recite the tool call"), instead weaving retrieved facts naturally into the next utterance.
**Evidence:** This is stated as explicit prompting-guide policy (primary source: OpenAI Agents SDK docs), not a study, so treat the specific millisecond cutoffs as OpenAI's design opinion rather than a validated number.
**Application to Yuki:** Adopt the "never recite the tool" rule directly for the talker's system prompt: the narration lines described in 2.2/2.3 should sound like Yuki thinking out loud ("let me open that email...") not like an API log ("calling send_email with params..."). Also adopt the 2s/5s thresholds as reasonable defaults for Yuki's own preamble/reminder timing pending its own measurement.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Prompting-only change, free.
**Sources:** [Voice Agents — OpenAI Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-js/guides/voice-agents/), [Pipeline — OpenAI Agents SDK](https://openai.github.io/openai-agents-python/voice/pipeline/) — **note:** the specific ms thresholds above were reported via a secondary engineering blog (Zylos Research) summarizing this guidance; verify against OpenAI's primary docs before hard-coding the exact numbers.

### 2.5 Contrarian data point: Sierra explicitly avoids filler-audio as a latency-gaming tactic
**What it is:** Sierra's voice engineering blog states they deliberately measure "time to first *relevant* response" rather than time-to-first-audio, calling out filler phrases like "let me check" as a way some vendors game perceived-latency metrics without actually improving the substantive answer time. They instead invested in a custom low-latency VAD model to cut real reaction lag.
**Evidence:** Sierra's own engineering blog (primary source, named company, technical detail on their custom VAD).
**Application to Yuki:** Important balancing consideration: progress narration (2.2/2.3) is good when it's *grounded in real events* (a tool actually started/finished), but generic filler that isn't tied to anything real risks feeling like padding rather than presence, undermining trust over time. Yuki's narration lines should always be conditioned on an actual tool-call state transition — never a canned "let me think" with nothing behind it — which also happens to be exactly what 2.1's shared-state design provides for free.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Design constraint, not a cost.
**Sources:** [Engineering low-latency voice agents (Sierra)](https://sierra.ai/blog/voice-latency)

### 2.6 Mid-task interruption/steering (soft interrupt + asyncio cancellation)
**What it is:** The emerging pattern across agent frameworks (Strands Agents' `Interrupts`, various "realtime steering" write-ups) for handling a user talking over a running multi-step task: rather than hard-killing the task, let the in-flight tool call finish, but feed the user's new utterance as fresh context into the *next* decision point of the agent loop — i.e., "soft interrupt with a steering queue" — using cooperative cancellation (asyncio) rather than a full teardown/restart.
**Evidence:** This is a converging pattern described across multiple 2026 engineering write-ups and is also an open feature request against Anthropic's own Claude Code (issue #30492) — i.e., even Anthropic's own agent tooling doesn't yet have a first-class solution, so treat this as an active, unsolved problem area rather than an off-the-shelf technique.
**Application to Yuki:** When the thinker is mid-UIA-task and the user interrupts by voice, Yuki should (a) let the talker acknowledge immediately ("got it, one sec" — via the barge-in/backchannel handling in 1.7), (b) not abort the current UIA action outright (which could leave the OS in a half-done state — e.g. a half-filled form), but (c) queue the user's correction as the next input the thinker consults before its next action. This is a meaningful engineering investment, not a quick fix.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Architecture work; no new external cost, but real implementation complexity (state machine for "safe interruption points" in a UIA action sequence).
**Sources:** [Realtime steering: Interrupt, barge-in, redirect, and guide the AI (Ably)](https://ably.com/blog/ai-transport-redirect-steering), [Interrupts — Strands Agents docs](https://strandsagents.com/docs/user-guide/concepts/interrupts/), [Claude Code feature request #30492 (open, unresolved)](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/30492)

**Flag on "gradient-bang":** The brief's premise names "gradient-bang by Pipecat" as a narration technique. Investigation found `pipecat-ai/gradient-bang` is real, but it is an unrelated **multiplayer LLM game demo** ("explore, trade, battle... with LLMs"), not a tool-call-narration pattern. There is no public Pipecat feature by this name related to progress narration — do not cite it as a technique; Section 2.3's actual primitives are the real equivalent.

## 3. Computer-Use Screen Understanding

### 3.1 Microsoft UFO2 — closest architectural analog to Yuki, and the strongest reference
**What it is:** Microsoft's own Windows "Desktop AgentOS": a centralized **HostAgent** (task decomposition/coordination) plus per-application **AppAgent**s, using a **hybrid perception pipeline that fuses Windows UI Automation (UIA) metadata with OmniParser-v2 vision-based grounding**, a unified GUI+native-API action layer, retrieval-augmented memory of past executions, and **speculative multi-action execution** to cut LLM calls per task. It also runs its automation inside an isolated **Picture-in-Picture (PiP) virtual desktop**, so the agent clicks/types in its own virtual session instead of fighting the user for the real mouse/keyboard/foreground window.
**Evidence:** Peer-reviewed/arXiv paper (April 2025) from Microsoft Research, with public docs and code (github.com/microsoft/UFO); this is the same institution and the same OS Yuki targets, and the same base signal (UIA) Yuki already captures. The paper reports "substantial improvements in robustness and execution accuracy over prior CUAs" across 20+ real Windows apps, though an exact accuracy delta isn't in the public abstract — treat the magnitude as directionally credible, not a precise number.
**Application to Yuki:** This is the single most directly transferable reference. Concretely: (1) UFO2's finding that pure-UIA fails on custom-rendered controls (canvas UI, some Electron/game-engine apps) motivates adding a vision fallback exactly where UIA returns nothing — not everywhere; (2) its "speculative multi-action execution" (batching several predicted UI actions per LLM call instead of one-action-per-call) is a concrete latency/cost lever for the thinker's UIA task loop; (3) its retrieval-augmented memory of prior executions overlaps with Yuki's own memory system and know-how store — worth reading the paper's memory design for ideas on structuring "learned procedures"; (4) **the PiP isolation idea is worth adopting on its own** even before any vision fallback — it directly solves the problem of the thinker's UI Automation actions stealing the user's foreground focus/mouse while they're doing something else, which a voice-first "lives in my PC" assistant needs regardless of perception method.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Would require running OmniParser-v2 locally as the vision fallback (see 3.2 for its footprint) only when UIA is insufficient — i.e., gated, not always-on, keeping it compatible with the "lightweight" constraint. The PiP virtual-desktop approach costs a small amount of OS-level session/window management, no GPU.
**Sources:** [UFO2: The Desktop AgentOS (arXiv 2504.14603)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14603), [UFO2 overview docs (Microsoft)](https://microsoft.github.io/UFO/ufo2/overview/), [Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/ufo2-the-desktop-agentos/), [microsoft/UFO (GitHub)](https://github.com/microsoft/UFO)

### 3.1a Accessibility-tree-first is a systems-level win, not just an accuracy one — the token/latency numbers
**What it is:** The general principle behind 3.1 (prefer UIA, escalate to vision only on failure) has a concrete, quantified systems argument behind it, not just an accuracy one: an accessibility tree typically serializes a screen to roughly **200-400 tokens**, versus roughly **3,000 tokens** for a single screenshot processed by a vision-language model — and switching a GUI agent from screenshot-based to accessibility-native execution has been reported to cut per-action latency from **~2-5s down to under 500ms**.
**Evidence:** "Efficient GUI Agents: A Systems Survey of Observation, Memory, Action, and Runtime Optimization" (arXiv 2609.02309); consistent with the per-action latency figures independently cited for Claude's computer-use tool in 3.7.
**Application to Yuki:** This is direct, numeric validation that Yuki's UIA-first design is the right default and should stay the default — the fix for "misses what's on the screen" is a targeted, gated vision escalation (3.2/3.4/3.6), not replacing UIA capture with vision. One caveat from the same survey: accessibility-tree extraction itself adds non-trivial wall-clock overhead over long multi-step tasks if the tree is re-walked on every step — cache/diff the tree between steps rather than re-querying UIA fresh each time.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Near-zero marginal cost (already local, already built) — this is the cheapest tier and the one to optimize for staying on, not off.
**Sources:** [Efficient GUI Agents: A Systems Survey (arXiv 2609.02309)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02309), InfoWorld: "Accessibility is the first-class interface for AI agents."

### 3.2 Microsoft OmniParser v2 — the vision-fallback component itself
**What it is:** A lightweight screenshot-to-structured-UI parser: a fine-tuned YOLOv8 icon/control detector plus a Florence-2-based captioner that names each detected element's function, turning a raw screenshot into a list of labeled, clickable regions any LLM can reason over (this is what UFO2 fuses with UIA).
**Evidence:** 0.6s/frame on an A100, 0.8s/frame on a single RTX 4090; 39.6 average accuracy on the ScreenSpot Pro benchmark (a hard benchmark with high-resolution screens and tiny targets); v2 cut latency 60% vs v1 by shrinking the captioner's input image size.
**Application to Yuki:** Directly usable as the fallback path when Yuki's UIA text capture returns nothing useful (custom-drawn UI, some games/canvas apps, certain Electron apps with poor accessibility trees). Given Yuki's RTX 2060 (per the user's known gaming-PC specs), expect meaningfully slower than the 4090's 0.8s/frame figure — should be benchmarked locally before committing, and gated to run only on-demand, never continuously, and never during games (per the user's hard constraint).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Local GPU inference, sub-second to a few seconds per frame depending on GPU; must be invoked sparingly (on UIA-capture-failure, not per-frame) to respect Yuki's lightweight/no-GPU-during-games requirement.
**Sources:** [OmniParser V2: Turning Any LLM into a Computer Use Agent (Microsoft Research)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/articles/omniparser-v2-turning-any-llm-into-a-computer-use-agent/), [microsoft/OmniParser-v2.0 (Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/OmniParser-v2.0)

### 3.3 Benchmark landscape: OSWorld / WindowsAgentArena — what's winning and why it matters for technique choice
**What it is / evidence (numbers, with sources conflicting where noted):**
- **Agent S3** (Simular AI) — introduces "Behavior Best-of-N" (multiple rollouts, pick the best), reported by Simular as **69.9%** on OSWorld in their announcement thread, vs. a separate Simular article claiming **72.6%**, both described as at/near the reported human baseline of ~72.4%. *(Flag: the two Simular-published figures for the same model disagree by ~3 points — likely different measurement dates/protocol revisions; treat "approaching human parity" as the safe claim, not a precise number.)* Progression was dramatic: Agent S (20.6%) → Agent S2 (48.8%) → Agent S3 (~70%) in about one year.
- **UI-TARS-2** (ByteDance) — a unified vision-language-action model (not a modular pipeline) scoring **47.5%** on OSWorld, reported to beat "strong Claude and OpenAI agent baselines"; UI-TARS-72B specifically scored 24.6% (50 steps) vs. Claude's reported 22.0%.
- **OpenAI CUA** — **38.1%** on OSWorld (full OS control), but much stronger on browser-only tasks: 58.1% WebArena, 87% WebVoyager — i.e., current computer-use agents are far better at web tasks than native desktop tasks.
- **WindowsAgentArena** (Windows-specific, 150+ tasks): Microsoft's own baseline agent "Navi" scored only **19.5%** vs. human 74.5%; the current best reported is **CoAct-1** at **52.5%** (vs. Agent S2's 29.8%, Navi's 19.5%), notably strong on system/utility tasks (83.3%/77.7%) — CoAct-1's edge comes from **writing and executing code as actions** rather than pure GUI clicking, a materially different technique from UFO2/Agent-S's GUI-action approach.
**Application to Yuki:** Three takeaways: (1) hybrid UIA+vision (UFO2-style) and wide-scaling/self-verification (Agent-S3-style) are the two techniques actually driving state-of-the-art gains, not bigger single-shot VLMs; (2) CoAct-1's "write code instead of clicking" approach is worth considering for the thinker for tasks that map cleanly to scriptable actions (e.g., file operations, batch renames) since it sidesteps GUI-grounding error entirely; (3) Windows-native tasks are measurably harder than web tasks for every agent — expect the thinker to need more retries/fallback logic on native Win32/legacy apps than on browser-based work.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Agent S3's Best-of-N approach multiplies LLM calls (multiple rollouts) — expensive, likely not appropriate for Yuki's per-task cost/latency budget except for high-stakes tasks; CoAct-1-style code-writing is comparatively cheap (one generation, direct execution) and worth prioritizing.
**Sources:** [Agent S3 announcement (Simular AI)](https://www.simular.ai/articles/agent-s3), [Simular's computer use agent outperforms humans](https://www.simular.ai/articles/simulars-computer-use-agent-outperforms-humans), [UI-TARS-2 Technical Report (arXiv 2509.02544)](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02544v1), [OpenAI Computer-Using Agent announcement](https://openai.com/index/computer-using-agent/), [WindowsAgentArena (Microsoft)](https://microsoft.github.io/WindowsAgentArena/), [CoAct-1 (arXiv 2508.03923)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.03923)

### 3.4 Set-of-Marks (SoM) prompting — useful only as a vision-fallback aid, not for UIA-covered UI
**What it is:** Overlaying numbered/labeled marks on segmented image regions before showing a screenshot to a vision-language model, so the model can refer to "button 7" instead of guessing pixel coordinates.
**Evidence:** Improved GPT-4V grounding accuracy on RefCOCOg from 25.7% to 86.4% (ACC@0.5) — a very large jump — but the same research notes SoM **decreases** performance on open-weight (LLaVA-family) models, i.e., it's model-dependent and mainly helps strong proprietary VLMs.
**Application to Yuki:** Relevant only for the OmniParser/vision-fallback path (3.2), and only if Yuki's fallback grounding uses a Claude vision call (proprietary, so SoM should help) rather than a small open model. Since UIA already gives exact coordinates/bounding boxes for anything it can see, SoM is irrelevant for the primary (UIA) path — it's purely a fallback-path accuracy booster.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Adds a segmentation preprocessing step before the vision call; OmniParser already effectively does this (it outputs labeled boxes), so this may already be "free" if the fallback path is built on OmniParser rather than raw screenshots.
**Sources:** [Set-of-Mark Prompting Unleashes Extraordinary Visual Grounding in GPT-4V (arXiv 2310.11441)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11441)

### 3.5 Windows built-in OCR (`Windows.Media.Ocr`) as a lightweight, no-GPU text fallback
**What it is:** A built-in WinRT OCR engine (`OcrEngine.RecognizeAsync`) that extracts text + word/line bounding boxes from a bitmap, entirely on-device, no model download, no GPU dependency for the legacy CPU-based engine.
**Evidence:** No latency/accuracy benchmark numbers were found in official docs (a genuine gap — flag as unverified for perf, needs local measurement). Note also: **a newer Windows AI text-recognition API exists that is NPU-accelerated and only available on Copilot+ PCs** — worth checking whether the user's hardware qualifies, since the two APIs likely differ substantially in speed/accuracy.
**Application to Yuki:** This is the natural "cheapest possible" fallback tier — below OmniParser (GPU-based) — for text Yuki's UIA capture misses on custom-rendered controls, useful specifically because it needs no GPU and thus never competes with games. Recommended tiering: UIA (primary, free) → Windows OCR (cheap CPU fallback for missed text) → OmniParser-v2 vision (GPU, gated, used only when the first two both fail to identify actionable elements).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** CPU-only, app must run with package identity (MSIX) for the legacy API — worth checking this constraint against Yuki's actual packaging. **Flag:** no hard latency numbers found; benchmark locally before relying on it in the critical path.
**Sources:** [Windows.Media.Ocr namespace (Microsoft Learn)](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/windows.media.ocr), [OcrEngine class docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/windows.media.ocr.ocrengine)

### 3.6 Chromium accessibility flags for browser content specifically
**What it is:** `--force-renderer-accessibility[=complete]` forces Chrome/Chromium to build and expose its full accessibility tree (normally built lazily/on-demand), which is what UIA-based tools actually read when inspecting a Chrome window.
**Evidence:** Documented Chromium flag; **note a known regression** — Chrome 117+ broke the flag when passed with no parameter; the workaround is to always pass `=complete` explicitly.
**Application to Yuki:** Since a large fraction of a typical Windows user's screen time is inside Chrome/Edge (Chromium-based), and Yuki's UIA capture depends on the accessibility tree being populated, this flag (or ensuring it's set for the browser Yuki instruments) is a prerequisite for reliable UIA text capture from web pages — worth explicitly verifying Yuki's current setup handles this, especially post-Chrome-117 versions.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Free; a launch-flag/registry setting, but building the full accessibility tree does add some overhead to the browser itself (Chromium's own docs note accessibility is off by default partly for performance reasons) — worth checking it doesn't measurably slow the user's browser.
**Sources:** [Chromium Accessibility Overview](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/accessibility/overview.md), [Chrome 117 breaks UIA mode using --force-renderer-accessibility (issue)](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/40072866)

### 3.7 Claude computer-use tool: cost/latency reference point, with current accuracy numbers for the exact model family Yuki uses
**What it is:** Anthropic's screenshot-based computer-use loop: the model requests a screenshot, gets an image back, issues click/type/scroll actions — no accessibility-tree input at all, purely vision+coordinates.
**Evidence:** ~2-5s per action; most tasks take 5-15 iterations (30+ for complex workflows); token cost is dominated by image inputs — a multi-step task can burn 50,000-200,000+ tokens, costing "a few cents to over a dollar" per workflow. On accuracy: Anthropic's own reporting puts **Claude Sonnet 4.6 at 72.5% on OSWorld-Verified** (a July-2025-revised version of OSWorld; not directly comparable to pre-revision OSWorld numbers cited elsewhere in 3.3) and **Opus 4.8 at 83.4%** — both above OSWorld's ~72-75% human baseline. *(Flag: some third-party "2026 OSWorld leaderboard" blog posts publish diverging numbers with no disclosed methodology — treat only vendor-published numbers with a named benchmark version as reliable.)*
**Application to Yuki:** Since the thinker is already Claude Sonnet 5 on Bedrock, the vision-fallback path is "free" in the sense of no new vendor integration — invoke the computer-use tool only on the UIA-miss path (3.1/3.1a), not as the default loop, given it's far more token/latency-expensive than a UIA read. The Sonnet/Opus OSWorld-Verified numbers above are a reasonable accuracy expectation for whatever fraction of Yuki's tasks fall through to this fallback.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** As above — this is the expensive path; keep it a fallback, not primary.
**Sources:** [Computer use tool (Claude Platform Docs)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool), [Introducing Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic)](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-sonnet-4-6)

### 3.8 Local vision-language-action models (UI-TARS, Agent S3 stack) do not fit Yuki's actual GPU — concrete hardware check
**What it is:** UI-TARS (ByteDance's native VLA GUI model, no external accessibility tree needed) is the most capable *locally-runnable* GUI agent model family, available in 2B/7B/72B sizes; Agent S3's reported near-human-parity OSWorld scores (3.3) depend on "Behavior Best-of-N" — running several rollouts per task and picking the best, multiplying inference cost.
**Evidence:** UI-TARS-1.5-7B requires roughly 16GB VRAM at FP16, or ~4GB with aggressive quantization; Agent S3's Best-of-N scaling multiplies per-task LLM/VLM calls by the rollout count (reported up to 10x) to reach its top scores.
**Application to Yuki:** The user's actual hardware is a Ryzen 2700 + RTX 2060 (6GB VRAM). Even the quantized 7B UI-TARS variant leaves little headroom, and any headroom it does leave is exactly what games need — directly violating the "never run heavy models during games/calls" constraint. Agent S3-style Best-of-N is a cost multiplier that doesn't map onto a "lightweight, always-available" assistant regardless of GPU. **Conclusion: rule out self-hosting any VLA-style GUI model on this machine.** The only GPU-based vision fallback worth using is an on-demand, gated call to OmniParser-v2 (3.2) for the specific failure case UIA can't see, or — cheaper still — Claude's own hosted computer-use vision call (3.7), since neither competes for local VRAM at all.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** N/A to adopt directly; this is a "do not build" finding specific to the deployed hardware, not a technique.
**Sources:** [UI-TARS-1.5 (ByteDance, GitHub/model card)](https://github.com/bytedance/UI-TARS), [Agent S3 announcement (Simular AI)](https://www.simular.ai/articles/agent-s3)

## 4. Proactive Assistants

### 4.1 Horvitz's Principles of Mixed-Initiative UI (CHI 1999) — the foundational framework, still the right lens
**What it is:** A 12-principle framework for when and how an autonomous agent should take initiative, including: model uncertainty about the user's actual goal; weigh the timing of an intervention against the user's current attention state; explicitly weigh the cost/benefit/uncertainty of acting vs. not; minimize the cost of a wrong guess (both wrong content and wrong timing); always give the user a clean way to invoke or terminate the agent's help; behave with socially appropriate norms.
**Evidence:** Foundational HCI paper, still actively cited/applied to LLM-agent interruptibility research in 2025-2026 papers.
**Application to Yuki:** This should be the design constitution for Yuki's proactivity, not just inspiration: concretely, (1) require a confidence/uncertainty estimate before any unsolicited interruption — Yuki should have a numeric or categorical "how sure am I this is worth interrupting for" gate; (2) explicitly model the user's current attention state (e.g., mid-meeting, mid-typing a long doc, in a game) as an input to whether/how to interrupt, not just whether something is *true*; (3) make "stop/undo/quiet mode" trivially easy and always available, since minimizing the cost of a bad guess matters more than maximizing the value of a good one.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Design principle, not a runtime cost — but implies building an explicit interruption-gating layer, which is real engineering work.
**Sources:** [Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces (Horvitz, CHI'99, full PDF)](https://erichorvitz.com/chi99horvitz.pdf), [People, Agents, and Interaction summary page](http://erichorvitz.com/uiact.htm)

### 4.1a Sensor-based interruptibility modeling (Fogarty & Hudson, CMU, 2003-2005; Horvitz's own "BusyBody," CSCW 2004) — old, empirically validated, and cheap to replicate without keystroke logging
**What it is:** A direct empirical follow-on to 4.1's theory: personalized statistical models that infer a real-time "cost of interrupting right now" from simple ambient signals — which application is in focus, how long it's had focus, time since last input — validated against secondary-task reaction time as ground truth for actual interruptibility (not just a guess).
**Evidence:** Their central, load-bearing finding is that these simple sensors predict human-judged interruptibility **better than human observers watching the same data** — i.e., you don't need sophisticated modeling or raw keystroke/mouse content to get a workable interruptibility estimate; foreground-app-identity and dwell-time already carry most of the signal.
**Application to Yuki:** This is the single most actionable, cheapest-to-implement item in this whole area, and it's compatible with the product's hard exclusion on keyboard/mouse *content* logging: Yuki already has foreground-app identity and window-dwell time from its existing UIA capture, so build a lightweight interruptibility score directly from that (e.g., low interruptibility during video calls, full-screen games, or sustained focus in one window; high interruptibility during idle desktop, browsing, or right after a task visibly completes) and gate proactive speech on it as the concrete implementation of 4.1's "weigh interruption cost" principle — this uses signals Yuki is not currently exploiting this way, requires no new capture, and needs no keystroke/content logging to work.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Essentially free — a small scoring function over data Yuki already collects; no new model, no new capture surface.
**Sources:** [Examining Task Engagement in Sensor-Based Statistical Models of Human Interruptibility (Fogarty et al., CHI 2005)](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2005.pdf), [Predicting human interruptibility with sensors (Fogarty et al., CHI 2003 / TOCHI)](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2003.pdf), [BusyBody: creating and fielding personalized models of the cost of interruption (Horvitz, Koch & Apacible, CSCW 2004)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1031607.1031690)

### 4.2 Proactive Agent / ProactiveBench (arXiv 2410.12361) — a trainable "should I offer help" gate
**What it is:** A dataset (6,790 real-activity-derived events, human-labeled accept/reject) and a fine-tuned reward model used as an automatic evaluator/gate for whether an LLM agent's proactive suggestion would actually be welcomed.
**Evidence:** The fine-tuned reward model reached F1 66.47% at predicting whether a proactive offer would be accepted — beating both open- and closed-source general models used zero-shot for the same judgment; note this means even a purpose-trained model is only ~2/3 accurate, underscoring how hard this problem is.
**Application to Yuki:** Rather than trying to hand-write heuristics for "should Yuki speak up now," this validates building (or fine-tuning a small local classifier as) an explicit accept/reject gate that sits between "I noticed something" and "I said something" — trained/tuned on the user's own accept/dismiss feedback over time (Yuki's coach/nudge system is well-positioned to collect exactly this signal).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** A small classifier call (or even a Haiku-based zero-shot judgment) per candidate proactive trigger; cheap, but adds a decision step before every unsolicited nudge — acceptable since these are not on the hot conversational-latency path.
**Sources:** [Proactive Agent: Shifting LLM Agents from Reactive Responses to Active Assistance (arXiv 2410.12361)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12361)

### 4.3 "Inner Thoughts" proactive conversational agents (CHI 2025, arXiv 2501.00383)
**What it is:** A parallel "inner thought" stream running alongside the overt conversation; the agent scores each inner thought with an **intrinsic-motivation** value and only surfaces it when that score crosses a threshold *and* a socially appropriate moment is detected — explicitly modeling the human experience of "I have something to say but I'm waiting for a good moment," rather than reactively predicting "whose turn is it."
**Evidence:** Peer-reviewed (CHI 2025); framed as an improvement specifically for the case where no explicit turn has been allocated to the agent (i.e., exactly the ambient/proactive case, not request-response).
**Application to Yuki:** This is a strong conceptual match for "I want it proactive 150%, like a human lives in my PC": implement an always-running low-cost "inner thought" evaluator over the ambient screen/activity stream that scores candidate observations, and only escalates to an actual spoken nudge when the score plus a "good moment" check (attention-state gate from 4.1) both pass. This effectively formalizes what Yuki's coach/nudge system should be doing under the hood.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Requires a continuously (or frequently) running lightweight scoring pass over new activity/facts — should be cheap (small-model or rule-scored), decoupled entirely from the real-time conversational pipeline so it never adds voice latency.
**Sources:** [Proactive Conversational Agents with Inner Thoughts (arXiv 2501.00383)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00383)

### 4.4 DiscussLLM — training a model to emit an explicit "stay silent" token
**What it is:** Instead of always generating a response, DiscussLLM trains the model to choose, at each point, between five intervention types (e.g., factual correction, concept definition) or a special silent/no-op token, using a synthesized dataset of multi-turn conversations engineered to contain natural "intervention triggers."
**Evidence:** Research-stage (2025 arXiv paper); the two-stage synthetic-data generation pipeline is the main contribution, not a deployed benchmark number.
**Application to Yuki:** The reusable idea, independent of the dataset itself, is the **explicit silence/no-op action as a first-class model output** rather than a bolt-on gate after the fact — i.e., bake "should I say nothing" into the same decision as "what would I say," rather than generating a candidate utterance and then separately deciding whether to suppress it. Cheaper and more consistent than a two-pass generate-then-filter approach.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Prompting-pattern change (add an explicit "stay silent" option to the decision space), effectively free.
**Sources:** [DiscussLLM: Teaching Large Language Models When to Speak (arXiv 2508.18167)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18167)

### 4.5 ChatGPT Pulse — proactive-by-batch, not proactive-by-interrupt, with an explicit feedback loop
**What it is:** OpenAI's proactive feature runs asynchronous research *overnight* (not live/continuous) drawing on memory, chat history, and connected apps, surfaces results as scannable topical cards the next morning, and lets the user thumbs-up/down each card to retrain what gets surfaced.
**Evidence:** Rolled out to Pro subscribers Sept 2025, expanding to Plus across 2026 — a shipped, real product, not a research demo.
**Application to Yuki:** Validates a *lower-risk* proactivity tier alongside real-time nudges: a scheduled, batch "here's what I noticed since we last talked" digest (drawing on Yuki's existing timeline/episodes/portrait) is much safer to get wrong than a live interruption, and the explicit accept/reject feedback loop is exactly the training signal needed for 4.2's gate. Consider building Yuki's proactivity in two tiers: (a) safe, batched/digest-style surfacing (Pulse-like) and (b) rarer, high-confidence real-time interruptions (gated by 4.1-4.3).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Off the real-time path entirely (runs on a schedule), so cost is whatever the underlying LLM summarization calls cost — not latency-sensitive.
**Sources:** [ChatGPT develops a Pulse (Platformer)](https://www.platformer.news/chatgpt-pulse-proactive-ai/)

### 4.6 Hardware-proactivity failures: Humane AI Pin / Rabbit R1 — lessons on *readiness*, not just *proactivity design*
**What it is:** Both products failed commercially; root causes per multiple postmortems: the core AI was slow and unreliable (not specifically "too proactive"), scope creep (trying to replace the phone entirely), and redundancy with the smartphone already in the user's pocket. Notably, the search did **not** surface documented evidence that "being too proactive" specifically was the cause of failure — the failures were about basic reliability and latency, with proactivity barely mentioned.
**Evidence:** Multiple 2025-2026 retrospective/postmortem articles converge on "the tech wasn't ready" and "confused novelty with product-market fit" as root causes.
**Application to Yuki:** The actionable lesson is inverted from what the brief assumed: the risk is not "Yuki will annoy the user by being too proactive" so much as "an unreliable or slow proactive feature erodes trust fast, and once broken, trust is very hard to earn back." This reinforces prioritizing the reliability/latency work in Sections 1-3 *before* significantly expanding proactive scope — a fast, correct, occasionally-quiet Yuki beats a chatty, sometimes-wrong one.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** N/A — a strategic/sequencing lesson, not a technique.
**Sources:** [Why Did the Rabbit R1 and Humane AI Pin Fail at Launch?](https://medium.com/@thcookieh/why-did-the-rabbit-r1-and-humane-ai-pin-fail-at-launch-c108d6e2bebb), [AI Product Failures 2026: Sora, Humane & Rabbit R1](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/ai-product-failures-2026-sora-humane-rabbit-lessons)

### 4.7 Screenpipe's event-driven "pipes" as a proactivity-trigger architecture
**What it is:** Screenpipe captures on meaningful OS events rather than polling at a fixed frame rate, and its "pipes" (markdown-prompt-defined agents) can be trigger-conditioned on behavioral patterns (e.g., "when I go to LinkedIn," or compound conditions like "when I answer someone on Slack and then check mail") in addition to being cron-scheduled, and can autonomously push OS-level notifications with click actions.
**Evidence:** Open-source, documented architecture (primary source: Screenpipe's own docs/GitHub); real, shipping (YC S26).
**Application to Yuki:** Directly reusable pattern for structuring Yuki's proactivity triggers: event-driven capture (already true of Yuki's UIA approach) + declarative, composable trigger definitions ("when X then Y") is a cleaner architecture than ad-hoc polling logic, and the "click action + per-pipe mute" notification design is a good template for making Yuki's nudges dismissable/tunable per nudge-type (satisfying Horvitz's "easy termination" principle concretely).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Event-driven (not polling) is inherently lower-overhead than fixed-interval capture; local-first, no GPU requirement for the trigger-matching logic itself.
**Sources:** [screenpipe architecture docs](https://docs.screenpi.pe/architecture), [screenpipe v2.07 blog (meeting detection, pipes)](https://screenpipe.com/blog/screenpipe-v2-07-native-macos-parakeet-meeting-detection)

**Flag:** Limitless (the Rewind successor) explicitly **does not** do inference-driven proactivity — per its own positioning, "it records passively rather than inferring, so it won't proactively suggest actions or anticipate needs." This is a useful negative data point: raw continuous capture alone (without an explicit inference/gating layer like 4.1-4.4) does not equal proactivity — it's necessary but not sufficient. Also flag: Limitless's Pendant hardware was discontinued for new customers following Meta's Dec 2025 acquisition — a live business-status fact, not a design lesson, but worth knowing before citing it as an ongoing reference product.

## 5. Memory for Workflow Understanding

### 5.1 Zep + Graphiti — temporal knowledge graph as the retrieval substrate
**What it is:** Zep's core engine, Graphiti, is a temporally-aware knowledge graph that ingests both unstructured conversational data and structured facts, explicitly tracking *when* facts were true (not just what's currently true) — directly relevant since Yuki's own fact/timeline system already needs to handle "this was true then, superseded later."
**Evidence:** On LongMemEval (a benchmark built for exactly this kind of long-horizon, temporally-aware memory), Zep reports accuracy improvements up to 18.5% over baseline and P95 retrieval latency under 200ms *without* an LLM call in the retrieval path; separately claims 94.8% vs. MemGPT's 93.4% on the Deep Memory Retrieval benchmark.
**Application to Yuki:** Graphiti's temporal-graph model is architecturally the closest published match to what Yuki's fact/timeline/episode/portrait system is already informally doing — worth reading the Graphiti paper closely as a reference schema (entities, relations, validity intervals) to formalize Yuki's own memory store, especially for the "screen-triggered recall" behavior the brief asks about (retrieving what changed/what was true last time the user was in a given app/context).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Sub-200ms retrieval (no-LLM path) is compatible with a live-conversation retrieval-augmentation use case; the write/ingestion side (turning raw facts into graph edges) is where cost would concentrate — likely fine if done async/off the hot path, matching Yuki's existing nightly portrait-rebuild pattern.
**Sources:** [Zep: A Temporal Knowledge Graph Architecture for Agent Memory (arXiv 2501.13956)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13956), [Zep Is The New State of the Art In Agent Memory (Zep blog)](https://blog.getzep.com/state-of-the-art-agent-memory/)

### 5.2 Mem0 — fast, low-token retrieval; but benchmark numbers conflict with Zep's own claims (flag)
**What it is:** A hybrid vector+graph memory layer emphasizing extraction efficiency (compact stored representations) and fast retrieval.
**Evidence — flagged conflict:** Mem0's own published benchmark reports it beating Zep on single-hop reasoning (LLMJudge 73.33 vs. Zep's 50.42) and running fast (0.71s P50). But this directly contradicts Zep's own published claim of beating Mem0 by ~22 points on LongMemEval (71.2% vs. 49%). **These are two vendors' self-reported benchmarks on different task subsets, each favoring themselves — treat both numbers with skepticism and, if this decision matters for Yuki, run an independent comparison on Yuki's actual data/query patterns rather than trusting either vendor's marketing benchmark.** A third-party comparison (Memori paper) put both below its own system on LoCoMo (Zep 79.09%, LangMem 78.05%, Mem0 62.47%), adding a third, again-different ranking.
**Application to Yuki:** Given the conflicting numbers, don't pick a memory backend on benchmark claims alone. The one consistent, useful signal across sources: Mem0-style "compact extracted facts" retrieval is genuinely lower-latency/lower-token than graph-traversal approaches when queries are simple single-hop lookups, while graph approaches (Zep/Graphiti) look stronger specifically on temporal/multi-hop reasoning — which matches Yuki's actual need (timeline + episodes + portrait = inherently temporal/multi-hop), tentatively favoring a Graphiti-like structure for anything temporal, with a fast flat-fact index for simple lookups.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Both are lightweight enough to self-host; the real cost driver is which one requires more LLM calls per write (extraction) — worth measuring directly against Yuki's actual fact-ingestion volume.
**Sources:** [Mem0's own benchmark blog vs. LangMem/Zep/OpenAI memory](https://mem0.ai/blog/benchmarked-openai-memory-vs-langmem-vs-memgpt-vs-mem0-for-long-term-memory-here-s-how-they-stacked-up), [AI Agent Memory 2026 — comparing 5 systems (independent, Medium)](https://medium.com/@wasowski.jarek/i-compared-5-ai-agent-memory-systems-across-6-dimensions-none-wins-6a658335ed0a)

### 5.3 Letta/MemGPT's three-tier memory model (Core / Recall / Archival) as an organizing schema
**What it is:** An OS-inspired memory hierarchy: **Core memory** — small, always in the live context window (like RAM), directly read/write by the agent every turn; **Recall memory** — full conversation history, searchable but outside the live context; **Archival memory** — long-term storage queried via explicit tool calls (like cold storage). The agent itself (via an LLM-driven "memory curator" step) decides per-turn whether to promote/demote/edit content across tiers.
**Evidence:** Well-documented, widely referenced architecture; "self-editing" quality is explicitly noted as a double-edged sword — adaptive, but memory quality depends entirely on the underlying model's judgment (no external ground-truth check).
**Application to Yuki:** Yuki's existing facts/timeline/episodes/portrait stack already resembles this tiering informally (facts ≈ recall, portrait ≈ core, timeline/episodes ≈ archival-ish); formalizing it explicitly against this three-tier model would clarify which layer is responsible for what, and specifically **what belongs in the talker's always-loaded context (core) vs. what the thinker should retrieve on demand (archival)** — directly relevant to the "misunderstands intent" complaint, since intent-misses are often a symptom of the wrong memory tier being in context at the wrong time.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Organizational/schema guidance; the "self-editing via LLM" pattern itself does add LLM calls per memory-write decision — Yuki may prefer deterministic/rule-based promotion logic for cost reasons rather than a full self-editing loop, given the lightweight constraint.
**Sources:** [Stateful AI Agents: A Deep Dive into Letta (MemGPT) Memory Models](https://medium.com/@piyush.jhamb4u/stateful-ai-agents-a-deep-dive-into-letta-memgpt-memory-models-a2ffc01a7ea1)

### 5.4 Task/activity-mining research for segmenting continuous capture into discrete tasks/threads
**What it is:** An established RPA/process-mining research area addressing exactly the "how do you turn an unbroken stream of screen/UI activity into discrete task boundaries" problem — e.g., identifying repetitive UI-log subsequences as "candidate routines," and more recent work using vision-language models to generate semantic activity labels directly from screenshots, aggregating event-level signals into higher-level activity segments.
**Evidence:** Multiple arXiv papers on UI-log segmentation for RPA routine discovery, plus a 2026 Springer paper specifically on VLM-based screenshot activity labeling for process logs.
**Application to Yuki:** This is the "what's next" for Yuki's memory pipeline that the brief specifically asks about: Yuki currently builds facts/timeline/episodes, but task/thread segmentation (grouping a scattered sequence of app-switches into "the user was working on the Q3 report" as a single addressable thread the assistant can reference later — "last time you were doing X") is a distinct, harder step that this literature directly targets. Concretely: adopt a segmentation pass that clusters timeline events into candidate task/thread boundaries (using recurring app+window-title+content patterns, similar to RPA routine-mining), then have episodes reference these threads rather than being purely chronological.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Can run as a periodic batch job (similar cadence to the nightly portrait rebuild) rather than live — no real-time cost; the VLM-labeling variant would need occasional screenshot+vision calls, which should be minimized/batched given the lightweight constraint.
**Sources:** [Identifying candidate routines for RPA from unsegmented UI logs (arXiv 2008.05782)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.05782), [Enriching Process-Related UI Logs Via Screenshot-Based Activity Labeling Using VLMs (Springer, 2026)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12599-026-00990-6)

### 5.5 Windows Recall's architecture is directly relevant — and its security failure is a direct cautionary tale
**What it is:** Recall's approach (continuous local screenshotting + on-device semantic indexing) is architecturally the closest shipping analog to what Yuki already does for screen capture, making its public post-mortems unusually relevant.
**Evidence:** Recall was widely criticized as "a built-in keylogger" at 2024 announcement; independent security researchers (including Google Project Zero) demonstrated extracting Recall's stored data **without admin privileges**, and preview builds stored screenshots in an **unencrypted** database; Microsoft's eventual fix (broad release, April 2025) involved isolating the feature in a VBS (Virtualization-Based Security) enclave and filtering sensitive fields (passwords, card numbers) before storage. Security concerns were still being reported as unresolved a year after relaunch.
**Application to Yuki:** Since Yuki already stores extracted screen text as facts, this is a direct, concrete warning: (1) encrypt the memory store at rest, don't assume "local-only" is sufficient protection against local malware/other-user access; (2) explicitly filter/redact known-sensitive field patterns (password fields, card-number-shaped strings) before persisting facts, mirroring Recall's post-backlash fix; (3) budget for the fact that "how is this secured" will be scrutinized by technically sophisticated users/press even for a personal, single-user tool.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Encryption-at-rest and field-level redaction are modest engineering additions, not a performance concern.
**Sources:** [Windows Recall (Wikipedia, well-sourced overview)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Recall), [One year after its rocky launch, Recall still raises security red flags (GeekWire)](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/one-year-after-its-rocky-launch-microsofts-windows-recall-still-raises-security-red-flags/)

### 5.6 Screen-triggered live retrieval pattern (synthesis, not a single citable source)
**What it is:** Combining 5.1's fast temporal-graph retrieval with 5.4's task/thread segmentation to answer, live during conversation, "what was I last doing here" the moment the user returns to a given app/window/document — this specific end-to-end pattern (continuous capture → segmented threads → sub-200ms retrieval → surfaced in the *live* voice turn, not batch) wasn't found as a single named product/paper; it is a synthesis of 5.1 + 5.4 + Screenpipe's event-driven trigger model (4.7).
**Application to Yuki:** Concretely: when Yuki's UIA capture detects the user has returned to an app/window/document matching a previously-segmented thread, fire a low-latency Graphiti-style query (sub-200ms, no LLM call) for that thread's last state, and hand the result to the talker as context *before* the user even speaks — this is what would produce the "I see you're back in X, last time you were doing Y" behavior the brief describes. This is a design recommendation synthesized from the pieces above, not itself a benchmarked technique — flag as the one item in this section that is proposed rather than found.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Depends entirely on 5.1/5.4's footprint; the live-path query specifically must stay sub-200ms and LLM-call-free to not add conversational latency.
**Sources:** N/A (synthesis) — see 5.1, 5.4, 4.7.

## 6. Human-Likeness in Voice

### 6.1 Gemini 3.8 Flash-Lite TTS's native style/emotion control — check this is actually being used
**What it is:** Yuki's primary TTS engine supports natural-language style-instruction prompts (tone, pacing, accent) plus inline bracketed emotion/delivery cues (e.g., `[whispers]`), and is explicitly positioned by Google as the fast/cheap tier for "conversational voice-agent pipelines" (as opposed to the Pro tier, tuned for maximum expressiveness at higher cost/latency).
**Evidence:** Announced Sept 2026 (Google blog + AI Studio docs); reported #2 on Hume AI's Overall Quality Index (a third-party TTS naturalness leaderboard) — one of the few concrete third-party quality signals found in this research area.
**Application to Yuki:** Since this is already Yuki's chosen TTS, the actionable finding is to verify Yuki's talker is actually *using* the style/emotion controls (varying delivery per emotional context — reassuring for errors, upbeat for good news, flat/quick for routine acks) rather than sending plain text with no style instruction, which would forfeit the model's main naturalness advantage over Aura-2.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** No extra cost — style instructions are part of the same request; "Flash-Lite" tier is explicitly the low-latency/low-cost option, appropriate for Yuki's needs.
**Sources:** [Gemini 3.8 Flash-Lite TTS (Google AI for Developers)](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/models/gemini-3.8-flash-lite-tts), [Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS and Flash-Lite TTS (Google blog)](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-3-8-text-to-speech/)

### 6.2 Deepgram Aura-2 fallback has a real expressiveness gap vs. the primary TTS — flag for fallback UX design
**What it is:** Aura-2's public controls are limited to **speed** and **IPA pronunciation override**; per Deepgram's own docs, its "expressivity"/emotion settings apply only to the newer Flux TTS product on `/v2/speak`, **not** to Aura-2 on `/v1/speak`.
**Evidence:** Directly stated in Deepgram's TTS voice-controls documentation — a primary-source, unambiguous capability gap.
**Application to Yuki:** Important operational flag: when Yuki falls back to Aura-2 (Gemini TTS outage/latency spike), it will lose emotion/style expressiveness, not just "sound a bit different" — the fallback voice will read flatter and more "assistant voice" than the primary. Worth deciding explicitly whether the talker should adjust its *text* (e.g., shorter, more clipped phrasing) when running on the fallback voice, since it can't lean on prosody control to carry tone in that mode. Also worth checking whether Deepgram's newer **Flux TTS** (not Aura-2) would be a better-matched fallback given it does support expressivity controls.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** No cost difference; this is a capability-parity gap to design around, not a performance issue.
**Sources:** [Speed, Pause, Pronunciation controls (Deepgram docs)](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/tts-voice-controls), [Emotional TTS in Live Voice Agents: Control Guide (Deepgram)](https://deepgram.com/learn/tts-emotion-control-live-voice-agents)

### 6.3 Disfluency insertion — genuinely improves perceived spontaneity, but reception is mixed (flag)
**What it is:** Research (arXiv 2412.12710) fine-tuning an LLM (via LoRA) to insert disfluencies ("um," "uh," self-corrections, trailing off) into otherwise-fluent generated text, then synthesizing with a disfluency-capable TTS model.
**Evidence:** A user study found disfluency insertion **significantly increased perceived spontaneity** — but reception was genuinely mixed: some participants found it more natural, others explicitly felt an assistant "should speak fluently" and found the fillers **inappropriate**. This is not a clean win; treat as a personalization/tunable-intensity feature, not a default-on behavior.
**Application to Yuki:** Given the mixed reception, this should be a low, sparingly-applied setting rather than pervasive — e.g., occasional micro-hesitations only in genuinely deliberative moments (recalling something, correcting itself), never in short factual acks, and ideally tunable/off-able per the user's own preference (Yuki's portrait/preference system could track whether this specific user likes it).
**Cost/latency/footprint:** If implemented via prompting (asking Haiku to occasionally insert natural hesitations) rather than a fine-tuned model, this is free; a dedicated fine-tune (as in the paper) is unnecessary engineering overweight for Yuki's needs.
**Sources:** [Enhancing Naturalness in LLM-Generated Utterances through Disfluency Insertion (arXiv 2412.12710)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12710)

### 6.4 Short-turn style + "acknowledge before answering" as the core anti-"assistant voice" techniques
**What it is:** Two related, well-established conversation-design practices: (1) keep spoken turns short — direct verbs, no stacked instructions, no essay-length answers, since spoken content (unlike text) disappears the instant it's said and can't be re-scanned; (2) separate **acknowledgment** (a quick continuer/agreement token signaling "I heard you, processing") from **answering** (the actual substantive turn) — conversation-analysis research formally distinguishes *continuers* ("mm," "uh-huh" — hold the floor, no semantic commitment) from *acknowledgment tokens* ("yeah," "okay" — claim comprehension) from full response turns, and voice-product UX guidance (Google's VUI design library, Vapi's prompting guide) independently converges on the same acknowledge-then-elaborate pattern, plus explicitly allowing banter/off-script remarks rather than treating every aside as an "off-topic violation."
**Evidence:** Google's own VUI design guidance and Vapi's production prompting guide both state this as house style; the conversation-analysis literature (backchannel/response-token research) gives the formal linguistic grounding for *why* it reads as natural.
**Application to Yuki:** This is squarely aimed at the "misunderstands intent"/"assistant voice" complaints: (1) enforce a hard brevity norm on the talker's default responses — reserve longer explanations for when explicitly asked "why" or "how"; (2) implement the acknowledge-then-elaborate split explicitly as two separate generation/TTS events rather than one long turn (this also directly supports the "talk while working" narration in Section 2 — the ack *is* the first narration line); (3) explicitly permit and design for banter/personality moments rather than only task-execution replies, since treating every non-task utterance as noise is itself a major contributor to "assistant voice."
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Prompting/turn-structure change; the two-event (ack + elaborate) pattern also directly helps latency (Section 1.6/2.2) since the ack can play near-instantly while the substantive answer is still generating.
**Sources:** [UI & UX Principles for Voice Assistants (Google Design)](https://design.google/library/speaking-the-same-language-vui), [Voice AI Prompting Guide (Vapi)](https://docs.vapi.ai/prompting-guide), [Backchannel (linguistics) overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backchannel_(linguistics))

**Counterpoint (freshly fetched, worth weighing against the brevity norm above):** Sierra's own voice-engineering blog argues turn length should be need-dependent, not uniformly minimized — "when agents give more detailed responses, people pick up the cue and share more context in return," i.e. a richer agent turn is used deliberately to prime the user into giving richer, more useful information back. This doesn't overturn the acknowledge-vs-answer split above, but it does mean "always be brief" is the wrong universal rule: Yuki's talker should default to short for acks/routine confirmations, but should be allowed to elaborate when the goal is specifically to draw out more context from the user (e.g., disambiguating an underspecified request) — matching the "misunderstands intent" complaint, where a too-terse clarifying question may itself be part of the problem. Source: [Building more human voice experiences (Sierra)](https://sierra.ai/blog/building-more-human-voice-experiences).

### 6.5 Persona consistency: bigger models drift more, and the fix is architectural, not just prompt-based
**What it is:** Research on "persona drift" — LLMs gradually deviating from an assigned personality over a long conversation, becoming generic, contradictory, or erratic. A notable, counterintuitive finding: **drift increases with model size** — larger models (e.g., LLaMA-3.1-405B, GPT-4o) drifted more across a conversation than smaller (7-8B) open models in one study, and drift correlates with certain conversational conditions (meta-reflection, "vulnerable user" patterns) rather than being a simple function of context length. A related finding: assigning a persona in the system prompt does **not** guarantee it's maintained — persona-consistency is not solved by prompting alone.
**Evidence:** Multiple 2025-2026 arXiv papers converge on this (identity-drift study, persona-drift review); mitigation approaches include contrastive-learning-based fine-tuning (COSPLAY-style reward for maintaining mutual persona coverage) and "boundary-aware" pipeline designs that explicitly re-anchor persona periodically rather than relying on it staying stable across the whole context window.
**Application to Yuki:** Since Yuki's talker is Haiku 4.5 (smaller/faster than Sonnet) this size-correlated drift finding is mildly reassuring but not a free pass — worth periodically re-injecting/refreshing persona-anchoring content (rather than trusting a one-time system prompt to hold for an entire long session), especially in longer sessions or ones involving meta-conversation about Yuki itself (which the research flags as a specific drift trigger). This is directly relevant to the "Yuki UI/voice direction" persona work already defined for the product.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** Periodic persona re-anchoring costs a few extra tokens per refresh cycle — negligible, especially combined with prompt caching (1.5).
**Sources:** [Examining Identity Drift in Conversations of LLM Agents (arXiv 2412.00804)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.00804), [Understanding Persona Drift in LLMs (EmergentMind survey)](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/persona-drift)

### 6.6 Sesame AI's "Crossing the Uncanny Valley of Voice" — the clearest applied thesis on what makes voice feel human
**What it is:** Sesame's public research framing (behind their Maya/Miles voice demos) argues human-feeling voice requires jointly modeling **prosody, tone, rhythm, and conversational context in one system**, rather than treating "what to say" (LLM) and "how it sounds" (TTS) as fully separate stages — plus sustained emphasis on emotional intelligence and **consistent personality** as core, not decorative, requirements.
**Evidence:** A widely-discussed public demo (viral reception, "uncanny valley crossed" reaction from press/HN/Reddit) backing the thesis, though the underlying claims are largely a company's own research framing rather than an independently peer-reviewed benchmark — treat the *framing* as credible/useful and the *specific virality* as marketing signal, not proof.
**Application to Yuki:** Since Yuki is architecturally cascaded (separate STT/LLM/TTS, not an end-to-end model like Sesame's CSM), full joint prosody-modeling isn't directly available — but the actionable takeaway is to treat prosody/tone as something the **talker's text generation should be aware of and steer**, not purely delegate to TTS after the fact: e.g., have Haiku emit both the words *and* a style directive (matching 6.1's Gemini style-prompt support) as part of the same generation step, closing some of the gap between a joint model and a cascaded one.
**Cost/latency/footprint:** No extra cost if folded into the same Haiku generation call (asking for a short style tag alongside the text); one extra field to parse, negligible latency.
**Sources:** [Sesame AI: $307M Raised, Maya/Miles, and the Quest to Cross the Uncanny Valley of Voice](https://timewell.jp/en/columns/sesame-ai), [Sesame AI Business Breakdown (Contrary Research)](https://research.contrary.com/company/sesame-ai) — **flag:** treat as a company's own research framing/marketing rather than an independently validated study.
